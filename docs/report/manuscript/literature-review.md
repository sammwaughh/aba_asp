## Causal discovery from observational data

Causal discovery addresses the problem of using data to infer, test, or refine causal structure, whether the relevant causal relationships are initially unknown or partly specified by prior domain knowledge. Such structure is important because causal knowledge supports explanation, prediction under intervention, and decision-making in scientific and practical domains [cite: glymour2019review, vowels2022d]. Although causal relationships are most directly established through interventions or randomised experiments, these are often infeasible: they may be too costly, ethically unacceptable, or impossible to perform. Under these settings researchers often work with observational data, sometimes supplemented by interventional evidence or expert domain knowledge, to identify causal structures compatible with the available evidence. This motivates the field of causal discovery: the inference, testing and refinement of causal graph structure from data and prior knowledge under explicit modelling assumptions.

A common representation for this task is a graphical causal model, in which nodes denote variables and edges encode causal hypotheses. Under the assumption that the graph contains no directed feedback cycles, such models are represented as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), where a directed edge \(x \to y\) represents the hypothesis that \(x\) is a direct cause of \(y\) [cite: glymour2019review]. Graphical causal models connect causal structure to observable statistical patterns. Under the assumptions of the causal Markov condition and faithfulness, d-separation in the graph corresponds to conditional-independence relations in the observed distribution [cite: glymour2019review]. Conditional-independence information can therefore constrain the class of causal graphs compatible with the data. However, it does not always identify a unique causal graph, since multiple DAGs may entail the same conditional independences and hence belong to the same Markov equivalence class [cite: glymour2019review].

Causal discovery methods differ in how they convert statistical evidence into graph structure. Constraint-based methods use conditional-independence tests to remove and orient edges. PC learns a Markov equivalence class of DAGs under causal sufficiency, while FCI extends the approach to settings with latent variables by returning a partial ancestral graph representation [cite: spirtes2000causation, glymour2019review, colombo2014order]. Score-based methods instead search over graph structures or equivalence classes using an objective function that trades off model fit and complexity, with GES as a standard example [cite: meek1997graphical, chickering2002optimal]. Functional causal model approaches use stronger assumptions about the data-generating process, such as non-Gaussianity or additive noise, to determine causal directions that may remain ambiguous under conditional-independence information alone [cite: shimizu2006linear, hoyer2008nonlinear, peters2014causal]. Rather than searching combinatorially over DAGs, Zheng et al. introduced NOTEARS which represents graph structure by a weighted adjacency matrix and replaces the discrete acyclicity requirement with a smooth equality constraint, allowing DAG learning to be posed as a continuous optimisation problem [cite: zheng2018dags]. These approaches show that causal discovery is methodologically diverse. Different algorithms impose different statistical, functional or optimisation assumptions to render causal structure identifiable and its estimation computationally tractable.

Despite this breadth, observational causal discovery remains strongly dependent on the assumptions and statistical procedures through which evidence is converted into graph structure. Constraint-based methods depend on the reliability of finite-sample conditional-independence tests; score-based and continuous-optimisation methods depend on the adequacy of their objective functions, regularisation choices, and search or optimisation procedures; and functional causal model methods depend on the correctness of their functional and distributional assumptions [cite: glymour2019review]. With limited data, erroneous or unstable evidence can therefore lead to inaccurate graphs as well as causal constraints that are difficult or impossible to satisfy simultaneously [cite: russo2024argumentative]. This motivates an important question: how should causal discovery proceed when the available statistical evidence is uncertain, defeasible, or inconsistent?

## Finite-sample uncertainty in constraint-based causal discovery

Constraint-based causal discovery has substantial theoretical support. In particular, Kalisch and Bühlmann prove uniform consistency of the PC algorithm for estimating the skeleton and equivalence class of very high-dimensional sparse Gaussian DAGs, showing that PC can be both computationally feasible and statistically justified under appropriate conditions [cite: kalisch2007estimating]. These guarantees rely on assumptions about the data-generating distribution, sparsity, faithfulness, and the behaviour of conditional-independence tests as sample size grows. Glymour et al. similarly emphasise that practical graphical causal search depends on assumptions, parameter choices, and finite-sample statistical decisions, and that search procedures generally do not provide confidence intervals for their selected structures [cite: glymour2019review]. These results establish that constraint-based causal discovery can be statistically well founded, but its practical output is mediated by the conditional-independence test outcomes obtained from finite data. This is potentially problematic because such outcomes can vary with sample size, test choice, significance threshold and modelling assumptions, so an algorithm may treat uncertain statistical outputs as reliable structural constraints.

Russo et al. analyse this issue by showing that finite-sample conditional-independence test outcomes can induce causal graph constraints whose joint satisfiability is not guaranteed [cite: russo2024argumentative]. In their rain, wet-roof, wet-street and watering-plants example, an erroneous reported independence, \(r \indep wp \mid \{wr\}\), conflicts with the ground-truth causal structure and prevents recovery of that structure when the reported causal relations are imposed simultaneously. In cases such as this the reported statistical evidence induces a set of causal constraints that no DAG can faithfully satisfy simultaneously.

Several approaches have sought to make causal discovery more robust to these finite-data difficulties. Within constraint-based discovery, Colombo and Maathuis address the order-dependence of PC-style algorithms by developing variants, including PC-stable and Majority-PC, that remove order-dependence from skeleton estimation and address order-dependence in edge orientation [cite: colombo2014order]. Claassen and Heskes use a Bayesian constraint-based approach in which input statements are assigned probabilities and higher-confidence statements can take precedence when conflicts arise [cite: claassen2012bayesian]. Hyttinen et al. take a symbolic approach, encoding causal discovery constraints in Answer Set Programming [cite: gel88] and selecting graphs by minimising the weighted cost of unsatisfied independence and dependence constraints [cite: hyttinen2014constraint].

These approaches address finite-sample uncertainty at different levels. Colombo and Maathuis modify the constraint-based procedure itself by removing or reducing order-dependence; Claassen and Heskes attach probabilistic reliability to causal statements; and Hyttinen et al. formulate causal discovery as optimisation over potentially inconsistent constraints. Rather than treating conflict primarily through procedural stabilisation, probabilistic weighting or optimisation penalties, argumentation-based causal discovery represents incompatible causal claims explicitly as defeasible assumptions whose acceptability can be evaluated under an argumentation semantics. The next section reviews this argumentation-based line of work, culminating in Causal ABA as the most direct framework for representing conflicting causal-discovery evidence.

## Argumentation-based approaches to causal discovery

Formal argumentation studies reasoning in the presence of conflict by representing arguments and attacks between them, with argumentation semantics determining which arguments can be accepted [cite: dung1995acceptability]. Assumption-Based Argumentation (ABA) [cite: bondarenko1997abstract] is a structured argumentation formalism in which arguments and attacks are generated from rules, defeasible assumptions and contraries. Attacks arise when an argument derives the contrary of an assumption [cite: toni2014tutorial]. This formalism is relevant to inconsistent causal evidence, since candidate causal claims may be supported by some statistical outputs and defeated by others. Russo et al. adopt this perspective by treating causal discovery as a setting in which conflicting causal assumptions can be represented and evaluated within an argumentation framework [cite: russo2024argumentative].

Explicit argumentation-based work in causal discovery remains limited. Bromberg and Margaritis address the unreliability of independence-based causal discovery from small datasets by treating conditional-independence test outputs as a potentially inconsistent knowledge base [cite: bromberg2009improving]. Their approach uses argumentation, augmented with preferences, to reason over independence and dependence facts related by graphoid axioms, producing an argumentative independence test that can improve the reliability of PC-style discovery. Russo et al. identify this work as a notable predecessor, but also highlight that some inconsistencies among reported independences and dependences may remain undetected [cite: russo2024argumentative]. This leaves room for an argumentation-based treatment in which causal graph constraints themselves are represented and evaluated within the argumentation framework.

Russo et al. develop Causal ABA as a richer argumentation-based framework for reasoning about causal graphs. Their method constructs an ABA framework from externally supplied causal information, including statistical and expert-driven inputs. The representation uses assumptions for conditional-independence statements and causal relations, while rules encode graph-theoretic constraints, including acyclicity and d-separation [cite: russo2024argumentative]. The central formal contribution is that causal constraints are internalised within a defeasible argumentation framework. As a result, alternative causal hypotheses can be evaluated through argumentation semantics, giving a principled connection between graphical causal discovery and non-monotonic reasoning [cite: russo2024argumentative].

Russo et al. instantiate this formalism computationally through ABA-PC. In this implementation, independence-test information from Majority-PC, one of the order-independent PC variants discussed above, is converted into independence or dependence facts and encoded as hard or weak constraints within a causal ABA framework. The system is implemented in Answer Set Programming and computes stable extensions corresponding to candidate graphs. When no stable extension exists, lower-strength facts can be dropped and the remaining compatible graphs ranked. The empirical evaluation compares ABA-PC with established causal discovery baselines on standard benchmark datasets, showing that Causal ABA is an executable approach to reasoning over uncertain causal evidence [cite: russo2024argumentative].

Causal ABA is therefore the most directly relevant prior framework for this project because it already shows how causal discovery evidence can be represented inside ABA and evaluated through argumentation semantics. Its scope is different from learning, however. The causal ABA framework is constructed from externally supplied causal information and then used to compute compatible graph hypotheses; it does not study how the ABA framework itself could be induced from examples. Russo et al. also note practical scalability limits, since grounding and extension computation are computational bottlenecks and the current implementation is restricted to small graphs [cite: russo2024argumentative]. The next section reviews ABA Learning, which addresses the separate problem of learning ABA frameworks from background knowledge and examples, and therefore provides the relevant machinery for asking whether causal ABA structures could be learnt or refined.

## ABA Learning and symbolic learning of defeasible theories

ABA Learning addresses the problem of how to augment ABA frameworks from background knowledge together with positive and negative examples. The aim is to extend an initial ABA framework so that arguments for positive examples are accepted while arguments for negative examples are not accepted, under a chosen argumentation semantics [cite: proietti2022learning, de2023aba].

A distinctive feature of ABA Learning is that learning is formulated as the transformation of an existing symbolic framework. Proietti and Toni introduce transformation rules, including Rote Learning, Folding and Assumption Introduction, as a way to extend background knowledge into an ABA framework that explains the examples under an argumentation semantics [cite: proietti2022learning]. Subsequent ABA Learning work retains this transformation-rule perspective while refining the learning setting and the semantics under which examples are accepted [cite: de2023aba, de2024learning]. The significance of this approach is that it supports intensional learning: the learnt framework can express general defeasible patterns through rules, assumptions and contraries, rather than relying only on example-specific facts.

The later ASP-based ABA Learning papers connect this transformation-rule setting to Answer Set Programming (ASP), a declarative logic-programming paradigm based on stable-model semantics. This connection is useful because stable extensions provide the semantics under which ABA examples are accepted or rejected, while ASP provides a computational implementation route for reasoning over such extensions. De Angelis et al. first studied cautious ABA Learning under stable extensions, where positive examples must be accepted sceptically and negative examples must not be accepted [cite: de2023aba]. Their later brave ABA Learning formulation instead uses credulous acceptance, requiring positive examples to be supported in at least one stable extension while excluding negative examples [cite: de2024learning].

The expressiveness of ABA Learning creates a separate search-control problem. De Angelis et al. emphasise that transformation rules can usually be applied in many alternative ways, rendering the space of possible learnt frameworks large and highly nondeterministic [cite: de2025greedy]. Greedy ABA Learning addresses this issue in case-based reasoning by imposing a deterministic transformation strategy: examples are first generalised exhaustively, and attacks are introduced only afterwards where they are needed to resolve conflicts. The paper demonstrates that ABA Learning may require domain-specific restrictions on transformation-rule application if it is to scale beyond small or unconstrained learning problems.

Causal ABA gives a formal account of how causal graph constraints can be represented and evaluated within ABA, while ABA Learning shows how ABA frameworks can be induced and efficiently computed from examples through transformation rules and stable-extension semantics. However, these strands remain largely separate. Causal ABA constructs its frameworks from causal-discovery inputs, while existing ABA Learning methods are not specialised for conditional-independence evidence, d-separation constraints, or causal graph structure. Greedy ABA Learning further shows that unconstrained transformation-rule search is difficult to control. These observations motivate the integration questions considered in the next section.

## Integrating Causal ABA and ABA Learning

The preceding sections identify a specific gap between Causal ABA and ABA Learning. Causal ABA provides an ABA representation for causal-discovery evidence: assumptions can encode candidate causal statements, rules can encode graph-theoretic constraints such as acyclicity and d-separation, and stable extensions can be interpreted as compatible graph hypotheses [cite: russo2024argumentative]. ABA Learning, by contrast, studies how an ABA framework can be extended or transformed from background knowledge and positive and negative examples, under the requirement that positive examples are accepted and negative examples are not [cite: proietti2022learning, de2023aba, de2024learning].

Adjacent work has considered non-monotonic causality in related but technically distinct settings. Giunchiglia et al. developed nonmonotonic causal theories for reasoning about actions, including indirect effects, nondeterministic actions, concurrent actions and defeasible causal laws, with implementation through the Causal Calculator [cite: giunchiglia2004nonmonotonic]. Billfeld and Kim study non-monotonicity in causal identification, using a context-dependent triangular-model approach that avoids strict monotonicity assumptions [cite: billfeld2024context]. Salmeron proposes Kolmogorov-Arnold Fuzzy Cognitive Maps, where scalar causal weights are replaced by learnable edge functions to model non-monotonic causal influence while preserving graph-based interpretability [cite: salmeron2026non]. These papers show that non-monotonic causal reasoning, causal identification and graph-based causal learning have been studied in other forms. They do not address the two-way interaction considered here: applying ABA Learning to causal discovery, and using causal structure to guide ABA Learning.

One direction is to apply ABA Learning to causal discovery. Causal ABA represents causal graphs within ABA by using assumptions for causal claims and rules for graph-theoretic constraints such as acyclicity and d-separation. Causal discovery can therefore be studied as a structured rule-learning problem, where causal relationships are induced from data within an ABA framework. In this direction, the object of learning may include causal assumptions, rule structures, or argumentative representations whose accepted extensions correspond to candidate causal graphs. The aim would be to assess how different ABA Learning strategies affect the quality, stability and interpretability of the causal structures obtained.

The reverse direction is to use causal structure to guide ABA Learning. Existing ABA Learning methods can involve large and nondeterministic transformation spaces, and Greedy ABA Learning shows that domain-specific restrictions may be needed to control this process. Causal discovery provides possible sources of such restrictions, including causal assumptions, causal ordering information, acyclicity requirements and graph-compatibility constraints. These forms of causal information could be used to constrain or prioritise transformations during learning. This direction would assess whether causal guidance improves the efficiency, robustness or interpretability of the ABA frameworks learned.

Together, these directions define the methodological gap considered by this project: how learning should enter a Causal ABA setting, what it should operate on, and how causal constraints affect the quality, stability, interpretability or efficiency of the learned causal or argumentative structures.
